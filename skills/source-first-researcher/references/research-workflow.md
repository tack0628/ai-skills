# Research workflow

Use this workflow proportionally. A narrow fact check may need one question and two sources; an article brief may need several evidence chains.

## 1. Frame the assignment

Capture the topic, purpose, intended downstream use, audience if relevant, geography, time range, language, freshness requirement, exclusions, and delivery depth. Define ambiguous terms operationally. List user-supplied claims as hypotheses until verified.

## 2. Build the question map

Assign stable IDs:

- `Q1...`: core questions required to fulfill the purpose;
- `Qx...`: contextual questions that improve interpretation but are not essential;
- anticipated claim types: definition, chronology, quantity, causation, policy, comparison, quotation, or interpretation.

For every core question, note the ideal primary source and a fallback. Prioritize questions whose answer changes the downstream conclusion.

## 3. Search from authority outward

Start with likely source owners and authoritative repositories. Use domain, file-type, title, identifier, date, jurisdiction, version, author, archive, and citation-chain searches as appropriate. Search secondary sources when they can reveal terminology, dissent, or missing primary material, then follow their citations upstream.

Log enough search coverage to explain important gaps. Do not produce a diary of every query unless requested.

## 4. Inspect and capture

Open the underlying source. For each retained source, capture:

- stable source ID and source class;
- author or issuing body;
- title and document type;
- publication, effective, revision, or data-period dates as applicable;
- canonical URL, DOI, archive identifier, case number, or other locator;
- access date in `YYYY-MM-DD`;
- supported points and relevant section, page, table, timestamp, or heading when available;
- limitations and relationship to upstream sources.

Prefer canonical URLs. If only an archived copy is available, record both the archive URL and original URL when known.

## 5. Verify claims

Create atomic claims: one proposition per claim ID. Compare claim scope with what the source actually supports. Check definitions, denominators, units, time windows, versions, jurisdictions, and whether language is descriptive, causal, mandatory, provisional, or promotional.

Use these labels:

- `verified fact`: directly supported by suitable inspected evidence;
- `reported claim`: accurately attributed, but not independently established;
- `inference`: reasoned conclusion from listed evidence;
- `opinion`: evaluative judgment attributed to its holder or explicitly offered as analysis;
- `unresolved`: evidence is absent, conflicting, inaccessible, or too weak.

Confidence (`high`, `medium`, `low`) is separate from label. Explain confidence briefly. Two dependent sources do not constitute independent confirmation.

## 6. Resolve conflicts and gaps

When accounts differ, compare date, scope, definitions, versions, methodology, proximity, incentives, and citation ancestry. Record the disagreement even if one source is stronger. Use `cannot determine` when the evidence does not support a resolution.

For missing evidence, record what would resolve the gap and the most promising next source or action. Never equate failure to locate evidence with proof of absence.

## 7. Stop responsibly

Stop when:

- every core question is answered or explicitly unresolved;
- consequential claims have suitable evidence or a visible warning;
- likely authoritative repositories and citation chains have been checked;
- new searches mostly duplicate existing evidence;
- remaining gaps are documented and further effort is disproportionate to the requested depth.

State important coverage limits. “Complete” means fit for the defined purpose and scope, not exhaustive knowledge of the topic.

## 8. Package for reuse

Follow `output-template.md`. Keep IDs unique within the package, map claims to questions and sources, and place drafting ideas only in downstream notes. The handoff must remain useful even if prose summaries are removed.

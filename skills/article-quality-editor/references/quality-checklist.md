# Quality Checklist

Use the questions that apply to the article and its stated purpose. A “yes” answer signals a possible finding, not an automatic defect; account for deliberate voice and audience expectations.

## 1. Factual safety and publication risk

- Are names, dates, chapter numbers, prices, statistics, quotations, titles, and technical terms unsupported or internally inconsistent?
- Does the article turn correlation into causation, interpretation into fact, or prediction into certainty?
- Are superlatives, “first/only/best” claims, or broad generalizations missing adequate support?
- Could time-sensitive claims have changed since the cited or supplied information was produced?
- Are quotations accurate, attributed, and short enough for the intended use?
- Are source quality and proximity appropriate for the importance of the claim?
- Does the draft contain legal, medical, financial, safety, privacy, reputational, or copyright claims requiring specialist or human review?

Never infer falsity solely from missing evidence. Assign an appropriate finding status from `review-format.md`.

## 2. AI-like expression and natural voice

- Could the opening, transition, or conclusion be pasted into an unrelated article unchanged?
- Does the draft repeatedly announce importance, surprise, or anticipation without a concrete reason?
- Are multiple paragraphs variations of the same point?
- Is the structure mechanically symmetrical despite uneven source material?
- Does excessive signposting slow the reader?
- Are abstract nouns and stock phrases replacing specific actors, actions, scenes, or evidence?
- Is sentence rhythm unnaturally uniform or the voice flatter than the strongest passages?

Describe the observable pattern and impact. Do not speculate about who or what wrote the text.

## 3. Redundancy, pace, and deletion candidates

- Does the lead reveal everything and then repeat it in the body?
- Does a later section recap information without adding evidence, interpretation, or consequence?
- Can two adjacent sentences or headings be merged without losing meaning?
- Is background repeated for readers who already received it?
- Are throat-clearing, meta-commentary, empty transitions, or generic conclusions removable?
- Does any heading contain too little substance to justify a separate section?

For every deletion recommendation, state what information, if any, would be lost.

## 4. Missing explanation and context

- Are unfamiliar terms, people, events, acronyms, or relationships used before they are established?
- Does the draft skip a reasoning step required to understand its conclusion?
- Are cause, consequence, scale, timing, or comparison missing where they determine meaning?
- Would the intended reader know why a quoted fact or scene matters?
- Does the article assume source knowledge inconsistent with its likely audience?
- Are counterexamples, uncertainty, or limitations needed to prevent overstatement?

## 5. Headings and structure

- Does the title accurately promise the article's content without exaggeration?
- Do headings describe their sections specifically and in parallel levels?
- Can a reader understand the argument or narrative by scanning headings?
- Is the order chronological, causal, question-led, or otherwise coherent for the material?
- Does each section answer the question raised by its heading?
- Are recap, evidence, opinion, and analysis separated when mixing them would confuse the reader?
- Does the conclusion synthesize rather than duplicate the introduction?

## 6. Reader clarity

- Is the intended reader and desired outcome clear from the draft or brief?
- Are pronouns, references, chronology, transitions, and comparison points unambiguous?
- Are sentences carrying too many claims or qualifications at once?
- Do paragraph breaks reflect changes in idea, speaker, scene, or reasoning step?
- Are technical detail and background calibrated to the reader rather than to the author?
- Does the draft answer the natural follow-up questions it creates?

## 7. Analytical depth

- Does the “analysis” section do more than summarize or react?
- Does each important interpretation identify its textual, factual, or logical basis?
- Does the article explain why the observation matters to the reader, argument, character, theme, or likely outcome?
- Are plausible alternative interpretations acknowledged where they change the conclusion?
- Are predictions calibrated and connected to evidence?
- Does the analysis add a distinctive, defensible insight rather than generic commentary?

## 8. SEO naturalness

- Is the primary topic clear without repeated exact-match wording?
- Do title and headings serve reader intent as well as discoverability?
- Are keywords inserted where synonyms or normal Japanese would read better?
- Does any sentence resemble a search query, keyword list, or template rather than prose?
- Are headings duplicated only to capture variants of the same keyword?
- Is the meta description accurate, useful, and consistent with the article?
- If internal links are present, are anchors descriptive and natural in context?

Do not claim ranking gains without evidence and do not invent an SEO target that the user did not supply.

## 9. Human verification queue

Before publication, isolate checks that cannot be safely completed from the available material:

- primary-source confirmation for high-impact factual claims;
- exact spelling, chronology, quotations, and attribution;
- current status of changing facts;
- permissions, privacy, copyright, conflicts of interest, or publication policy;
- specialist review for consequential legal, medical, financial, or safety guidance;
- destination and relevance of links whose contents were not inspected.

Each queued item should say what to check, why it matters, and what source or person can resolve it.
